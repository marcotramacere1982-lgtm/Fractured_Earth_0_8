# Narrative & Exploration {#ch-6}

## 6. Interacting with the world

Narrative and exploration phase

## 6-1. Passage of Time & Downtime {#sec-6-1}

The Narrative Phase encompasses all gameplay occurring outside of active combat engagements. It persists until an Operator initiates a hostile action, triggers a security alarm, or enters a high-threat zone that demands tactical precision. During this phase, action flows organically without rigid turn order, granting Operators the freedom to investigate environments, interact with NPCs, negotiate, and execute non-combat objectives at their own pace. While standard combat actions are not tracked during narrative play, executing specific tasks may still consume finite personal or team resources (such as credit funds, tools, or physical consumables).

### Passage of Time & The Operating Clock

To maintain realistic pressure during time-sensitive operations, narrative time is measured against an **Operating Clock** divided into standard **10-second intervals** (the equivalent duration of 1 tactical combat round). When Operators attempt tasks under a running clock—such as bypassing a security grid before a sweep, picking a lock ahead of a patrol, or downloading raw paydata before a connection drops—the GM assigns a specific time cost measured in these 10-second increments to determine how many rounds elapse in the background.

### Skill Checks & Difficulty Resolution

When an Operator attempts a challenging or uncertain task, resolution is determined by rolling a standard **d20** against the relevant **Skill or Attribute Target Number (TN)**, modified by the GM's declared **Global Difficulty Tier**:

- **Degree of Success / Failure:** Outcome quality is directly tied to the margin of the roll. Narrowly failing a check by 1 point may still yield a "success with a cost" or partial progress, while beating a check threshold by 10 or more points can grant unexpected mechanical or narrative bonuses (e.g., discovering extra paydata, reducing time spent, or gaining leverage).

- **Critical Hits & Critical Failures:** Rolling a natural 20 or a natural 1 does not guarantee an automatic logical outcome (e.g., a natural 20 will not allow an Operator to jump across a 100-foot gap). Instead, the GM determines the final success or failure based on the overall difficulty, but a Critical result always triggers additional narrative or environmental consequences—such as gaining permanent access privileges or triggering a minor local glitch.

- **Automatic Outcomes (Useless Checks):** If an Operator's effective Skill TN is far higher than the required difficulty, the GM should waive the check entirely to maintain narrative momentum. Conversely, if a task is logically impossible or the difficulty far exceeds an Operator's capabilities, no roll is permitted; the action simply fails.

### Downtime, Debt & Maintenance

Extended periods between operations allow Operators to enter a **Downtime Phase**, during which narrative time shifts from 10-second operational loops to days, weeks, or months:

- **Debt Interest & Collections:** Special Debts and loan terms accrue interest or trigger active collector intervention if minimum payments are missed between operations.

- **Aging & Physical Decay:** Extended downtime triggers mandatory aging and physical degradation checks, updating baseline stats as time advances across an Operator's career.

- **Restoration & Maintenance:** Downtime provides the required window to repair damaged cyberware, clear severe injuries, recalibrate overused hardware, and convert physical cash into gear or lifestyle upgrades.

## 6-2. Skill Checks & Difficulty Thresholds {#sec-6-2}

In *Fractured Earth*, any action with an uncertain outcome or high operational risk is resolved using a **d20-based Skill Check**. Every roll tests an Operator's natural capability or specialized training against a Target Number (TN) set by the Game Master, adjusted for environmental and situational complexity.

### The Universal Resolution Formula

To perform a check, the player rolls a single **d20** and compares the result directly to their **Effective Target Number (TN)**. An action succeeds if the d20 result is **less than or equal to** the Effective TN:

> **Effective TN = Base Stat or Skill Value - Global Difficulty Tier**

> - **Under / Equal Success:** Rolling ≤ Effective TN succeeds.

> - **Over Failure:** Rolling > Effective TN fails.

### The Global Difficulty Matrix

Game Masters do not calculate complex situational math on the fly. Instead, all environmental hazards, visibility issues, physical hindrances, and enemy defenses are abstracted into a single **Global Difficulty Tier** (from +6 down to −10), applied directly to the player's base metric:

::table[difficulty]

**Easier than Routine.** Not every scene works against the Operators. When the situation is on their side, the GM moves the scale the other way: **Favourable +2, Easy +4, Very easy +6**. This is how help works — there is no separate rule for it. Two average people giving each other a leg-up over a wall is *Easy*; one teammate steadying the ladder is *Favourable*; the whole crew hauling a crate up a ramp with the right gear is *Very easy*. Good tools for the job, plenty of time, or a tip from a contact count the same way. Pick one step for the whole situation (they don't stack with each other), and only the person rolling makes the check. The GM can set it for the whole table, or simply tell the player which step to pick in the roll window.

### Contested Checks (Opposed Resolution)

When two active characters directly oppose each other—such as an Operator attempting to sneak past an alert guard's perception, or a Hacker breaching a deck defended by an active NetRunner—resolution uses a **Contested Check**:

- **Both Entities Roll:** Each participant rolls a d20 against their respective Skill or Attribute TN (factoring in any relevant difficulty tiers).

**Determining the Winner:**

- **Single Success:** If only one participant succeeds their roll (≤ TN), that entity wins the contest immediately.

- **Dual Success (Margin of Success):** If both participants roll under their TN, the winner is the entity who succeeded with the highest margin of success (calculated as Effective TN - d20 Roll).

- **Dual Failure:** If both participants fail their rolls (> TN), the situational status quo remains unchanged, or the action results in a chaotic neutral stall (e.g., both hackers lock each other out temporarily).

### Helping a crewmate {#sec-6-2-help}

One crewmate can help another's check — if they can say **how**: holding the light, reading out the schematic, keeping watch, talking the guard's ear off. The helper rolls a skill that fits what they do:

- **Success:** the other gets **+2 TN** on their next skill or attribute check.
- **A natural 20:** they got in the way — **−2 TN** instead.
- **Any other failure:** nothing changes.

Only **one helper** at a time: a second one adds nothing. In a fight, helping costs the helper an action. At the table, *🤝 Help a crewmate* (beside your Strain) picks who and how, rolls, and the bonus waits on their sheet — it shows as its own row in their next check window, and a new scene clears it.

### Group checks {#sec-6-2-group}

When the whole crew does the same thing — sneaking through a lobby, climbing a fence, keeping a cover story straight — **everyone rolls**, and **the group succeeds if at least half of them succeed** (2 of 3, 2 of 4, 3 of 5). Strong members carry weak ones, but not forever. Help works inside a group check. At the table the GM asks for the roll as a *Group check*: your card says so, and the GM gets the tally when the last of you has rolled.

### Passive Checks & Static Thresholds

When an Operator is not actively declaring an action, but their background awareness or passive training might notice a hidden threat (such as spotting a dormant floor mine, sensing an ambush, or catching a lie), the GM uses a **Passive Threshold**:

**Passive Score = Base Skill Value - 5**

If an environmental hazard or an NPC's stealth roll is worse than the Operator's Passive Score, the GM quietly alerts the player to the detail without requiring a roll, keeping the narrative flowing smoothly.

## 6-3. Action Flow & Roleplaying {#sec-6-3}

Out-of-combat scenes in *Fractured Earth* are designed to be fast, grounded, and narrative-driven. Roleplaying should reflect the brutal, high-stakes nature of a dystopian cyber-future—where information is a currency, corporate surveillance is omnipresent, and every decision carries physical or financial consequences. To keep non-combat scenes engaging and prevent sessions from stalling in endless planning loops, both players and Game Masters should follow three core pacing principles:

### 1. The Intent & Means Protocol

When an Operator declares an out-of-combat action, the player must clearly state two things before any die is rolled:

- **Intent:** What is the exact goal you are trying to achieve? *(e.g., "I want to get the gang lieutenant to reveal the location of the paydata crate.")*

- **Means:** How are you using your character's skills, stats, or environment to achieve it? *(e.g., "I'm using my Streetwise/Dealing skill to offer him a high-grade military ammo clip as a bribe.")*

> By pairing Intent with Means, the GM can immediately determine if the action requires a Skill Check, automatically succeeds due to leverage, or fails because the difficulty is absurd. It eliminates vague declarations like *"I roll Persuasion"* or *"I hack the terminal."*

### 2. Momentum & Failure Forward

In the Narrative Phase, a failed roll should **never** result in a total dead end where nothing happens. Instead, every failed check pushes the narrative into a new, more dangerous state:

- **Success with a Cost:** Narrowly missing a check threshold means the Operator gets what they wanted, but triggers an immediate complication—such as raising the local security alert level, incurring a minor debt, or losing a valuable tool.

- **Complication Forward:** A total failure changes the situation entirely—the contact panics and locks the door, an emergency patrol enters the room, or the cyberdeck suffers a minor thermal flare. The story always moves forward; the environment simply gets harder to manage.

### 3. Framing Scenes & Pacing the Operating Clock

To maintain tension outside of combat, the GM frames narrative scenes like scenes in a tactical thriller:

- **Enter Late, Leave Early:** Skip mundane travel or minor conversation unless something is actively at stake. Cut directly to the moment of friction—standing at the door of a black-market fixer, lurking outside an encrypted corporate node, or ducking under a security camera sweep.

- **Leveraging the Operating Clock:** When players are investigating or planning a heist, the GM can start a **10-second Operating Clock**. Every decision, scan, or physical action advances the clock. Knowing that security sweeps or thermal lockouts are approaching forces players to make quick, imperfect choices rather than spending two real-time hours agonizing over a single door lock.

## 6-4. Information & Network Warfare {#sec-6-4}

Before weapons are drawn or alarms trigger, Operators often have a brief window to assess threats, manipulate local electronics, or slip into advantageous positions. Rather than executing an endless chain of standard skill checks during exploration, high-stakes pre-combat setup is handled through the **Prep Phase (Zero-Round)**.

### Skill Checks vs. Abbreviated Mechanics

During standard exploration, general tasks—such as scouting a hallway, picking a mechanical lock, or searching a local subnet—are resolved using standard **Skill Checks** (e.g., *Computers/Hacking*, *Stealth*, or *Perception/Awareness*).

However, when Operators actively prepare for an impending breach, ambush, or firefight, they may use specific class abilities or utility maneuvers. To avoid cluttering narrative play with combat rules, these prep actions are gated by the GM's assessment of time and environmental awareness.

> Combat abilities are generally not used in non-combat scenarios, but the GM may decide otherwise if the situation suits.

### The Prep Phase (The "Zero-Round")

When a confrontation is inevitable—such as guards approaching a heavy door, an enemy patrol sweeping into a corridor, or an Operator breaching a secured server room—the GM transitions the scene into a **Prep Phase**.

- **GM Action Allowance:** Based on how much advance warning or time the Operators have created (e.g., distant footsteps vs. an incoming breached door), the GM dictates how many **Prep Actions** (typically 1 to 3) each Operator may perform before Initiative is rolled.

- **Fresh Action Pool:** Prep Actions are completely independent. They **do not** consume or deduct from an Operator's regular Action Allowance (`ACTIONS / RD`) during Round 1 of combat.

### Typical Prep Actions

Depending on the time granted by the GM, Operators can execute critical setups across physical and digital space:

- **Digital & Network Prep:** A Hacker uses *Ping Sweep* to scan local subnets (what hangs on them, never where — 7-4), name the turrets on them, or pre-load a *Keylogger Injection* before enemies enter line of sight.

- **Spatial & Cover Setup:** A Soldier or Smuggler uses an action to move into **Full Cover**, take up a **Defensive Stance**, or pre-activate *Brace & Dig In*.

- **Environmental Rigging:** A Tech Wizard spends prep actions to deploy *Improvised Cover*, survey structural failure points (*Scavenge & Survey*), or rig nearby wiring (*Rig Power Conduit*).

- **Positioning & Ambush:** Stealthy Operators use prep actions to fade into dark corners, establishing **Partial / Full Cover** bonuses or preparing reaction triggers.

- **A toy drone:** anyone can carry a *Micro-Drone Recon Cam* — a palm-sized camera that flies for a few minutes. Out of a fight only (you either act or fly it): *Fly* on its Gear row, pick **Recon** (fly ahead and look — the GM tells you what it sees) or **Decoy** (draw eyes and ears away), and roll **Drones / Remote Craft**. Success: there and back. Failure: noticed, or it saw little — but it comes back. Failure by 5 or more: spotted and shot down, gone. Then its battery is flat: *Recharge* when the scene changes. Inside a guarded site the nearest network notices something in the air (+1 Trace, 7-4).

### Surprise & Alarms

If the party achieves complete surprise (e.g., ambushing an unaware patrol), the Operators gain a full Prep Phase plus a **Surprise Round** where targets cannot take reaction actions. Conversely, if an Operator fails a critical prep check (such as triggering an alarm mid-hack), the Prep Phase terminates immediately, alarms sound, and all participating entities roll standard **Initiative**.

**At the live table** the GM opens the Prep Phase with *Head start* (decision 233): your sheet enters the fight at **round 0** with the prep actions you were given (the usual get-ready window first). Spend them in any order — the enemies don't act; *Move* walks your squares; stances, cover, pings and abilities work as in a fight and carry into round 1. Then you roll initiative, and round 1 starts with a fresh pool of actions. With complete surprise your sheet says *Surprise round* through round 1.

### The site's alert {#sec-6-4-alert}

Modern security doesn't run on guards alone. Patrols wear AR feeds and radios, cameras and sensors stream to a local AI, and somebody reads what the AI flags. Every site the GM puts on the table has **one alert level** for all of that — *Calm*, *Twitchy*, *Alert*, *Lockdown* — and the GM turns it, by what you do: a guard who stops checking in, a zone that goes dark, a forced door, gunfire near a microphone. Pings and drones are heard by the networks and push it by themselves.

Cameras, motion sensors and laser lines report to the GM when you walk into them — unless they are off, cut from their network or looped by a hacker first. You don't see the dial. You see the site change: guards stop wandering and watch their posts, patrols go in pairs, a technician comes to look at the camera you blinded, doors you walked through a minute ago are now locked. A hacker's strong ping reads the mood.

**On Alert, quiet work gets harder.** While you stand on that map, **Stealth**, **Sleight of Hand**, **Electronics & Security** and **Computers & Hacking** are at **−1 TN**, and a Hacker's **Class TN** rolls too; on **Lockdown, −2**. Your check window shows it as its own row (*Site · On Alert −1*). The level never drops by itself: only time, a long way off, or a good story calms a site down.

## 6-5. Economy, Debt & Social Leverage {#sec-6-5}

In *Fractured Earth*, financial power is split between the oppressive digital track of corporate Credit Score (CRS) and the dangerous, off-grid economy of Physical Cash ($). Understanding the dynamic between these two systems is critical for negotiating, gearing up, and surviving between operations.

### Credit Score (CRS): The Debt Leash

Credit Score is **not** liquid currency—it represents an Operator's financial credibility, corporate standing, and active debt allowance.

- **Everything is Debt:** Corporate transactions (purchasing legal gear, renting safehouses, subscribing to med-services, or securing loans) do not "spend" credits. Instead, they attach debt leverage to your identity, directly lowering your baseline CRS.

- **Asset Security vs. Credibility:** Acquiring high-tier assets (e.g., buying a military-grade rifle) incurs a debt obligation that drops your CRS. The higher your CRS, the more line-of-credit you have to finance heavy gear, access restricted sectors, or leverage corporate contacts.

- **CRS Checks:** When trying to bribe mid-tier corporate guards, bypass automated security tolls, or gain entry to exclusive clubs, Operators roll against their **Credit Score**. A low CRS marks an Operator as a high-risk vagrant, making high-society social leverage nearly impossible.

### Physical Cash ($): The Black-Market Premium

Physical cash is off-grid, untraceable, and extremely dangerous. Because handling physical paper currency or gold bullion carries severe legal penalties and corporate prosecution risks, black-market vendors demand a massive surcharge to transact in cash.

- **The Cash Inflation Multiplier (3x to 10x):** Official item prices listed in databases reflect standard corporate credit transactions. Purchasing the exact same item on the street using untraceable Physical Cash costs **3x to 10x the base listed price** (determined by the GM based on local contraband risk).

- **What things cost:** see *Paying for Things* (8-1). The Credit Score cost of an official purchase grows quickly with how much attention the item draws, and the rarest gear (rarity A–B) is street-only.

- **Zero System Trace:** While exponentially more expensive, paying in cash leaves no digital debt trail, preventing mega-corps or bounty trackers from tracing gear acquisitions back to your identity or lowering your CRS.

### Bribing & Social Leverage

When attempting to persuade, intimidate, or bribe NPCs during the Narrative Phase, Operators must choose their leverage carefully:

- **Corporate / Official Contacts:** Require CRS checks or digital credit transfers. Offering physical cash to a corporate wage-worker may panic them or cause them to report an illegal transaction attempt.

- **Street / Underground Contacts:** Reject corporate credit (which can be frozen or traced instantly by authorities). Fixers, underworld dealers, and gang lieutenants operate almost exclusively on physical cash, trade goods, or heavy favors (*Call in Favor*).

### Managing Special Debts & Downtime

Failing to make minimum debt payments or letting your CRS drop to rock bottom triggers active debt enforcement. During Downtime, loan sharks, corporate repo squads, or bounty hunters will actively intervene, forcing Operators into dangerous high-risk heists simply to clear their interest and keep their heads above water.

## 6-6. Moral System & Corporate Friction {#sec-6-6}

In *Fractured Earth*, traditional moral frameworks do not exist. Society is deeply transactional, driven by corporate apathy, desperate survival, and cold pragmatism. Operators are rarely offered a choice between "right" and "wrong"—instead, they are routinely forced to choose between two bad outcomes, deciding who pays the price and who collects the payout.

Rather than tracking abstract alignment scores, ethics in game mechanics are handled through two direct operational realities: **Corporate Friction** and **The Unity Protocol**.

### 1. Corporate Friction & Faction Apathy

Factions, megacorps, and street syndicates do not view Operators through a moral lens; they view them as liabilities, assets, or statistical noise.

- **Transactional Relationships:** Fixers and corps do not care how a heist is executed, provided the paydata is secured and non-disclosure parameters are met.

- **Faction Friction:** Actions always generate friction with opposing interest groups. Executing a clean breach against a corp node might earn a short-term payout from a rival fixer, but it increases local security sweeps, raises corporate debt markers, or burns corporate contacts.

- **Unexpected Consequences:** In a heavily connected digital grid, "collateral damage" or "unintended favors" ripple outward. Leaving a low-level worker alive might preserve your conscience, but if that worker logs the breach, your team's Special Debt threat level spikes automatically.

### 2. The Unity Protocol (Crew Trust & Unity Checks) {#sec-6-6-unity}

Because the world outside is cold and transactional, an Operator's only real protection is their crew. The crew shares one **Crew track** from 0 to 10, starting at 5.

> **The Crew track measures whether the crew acts together — never whether the act was good.** A crew that agrees to execute a hostage gains trust exactly like a crew that agrees to free him. Only going against the crew costs anything. Play your character boldly: nobody is judging you as a person.

**What moves it:**

- **+1 — committed intent.** The crew backs a costly or risky call together: everyone goes back for the secondary objective, everyone stays to pull a teammate out. *Intention counts, not results*: it rises whether it worked or not.
- **+1 — the GM's call.** Moments of real crew spirit: covering for each other, sharing a payout fairly, paying a teammate's debt instalment.
- **−1 — override.** A player goes ahead against a crew that objected.

**What it does:**

- **Tight (7–10):** once per mission, a **crew favour** — someone takes a hit for you, shares a consumable or covers an instalment (the GM rules it).
- **Steady (4–6):** a working crew. No special effect.
- **Fractured (1–3):** no help from each other in combat; payouts get argued over; crew downtime events turn sour.
- **Broken (0):** the crew has split — a betrayal or a parting of ways becomes a mission hook.

**The Unity Check.** When a player declares a divisive, high-stakes intent — executing a captive, double-crossing a fixer, triggering an alarm — the GM opens a Unity Check:

1. The intent appears on everyone's sheet: *"Kira wants to: terminate the hostage."*
2. **The crew talks it through at the table.** This is the scene — take the time it needs.
3. Each other player locks a **secret vote** on their sheet: **Back**, **Object** or **Stay out**. Votes can change until the GM reveals them.
4. The GM reveals the **tally only** — *Back 2 · Object 1 · Stay out 1* — never who voted what.
5. The acting player decides. They are always free to go ahead. If they do so against a crew that objected, the track drops by 1; if the crew backed them, it rises by 1.

Tables that prefer it can vote by voice: the GM simply records the outcome.

### 3. Drives {#sec-6-6-drives}

Every Operator has a **Drive** — not a morality, a direction:

- **Justice:** rage at the system. Someone has to make it right, or make it pay.
- **Ambition:** wanting more for yourself and your own. Out of the gutter, whatever it takes.

Add a personal line in your own words — *"No kid ends up like my brother."*, *"I'll never be poor again."* — picked from the suggestions or written yourself, when you finish your character (and editable on the Dossier).

A Drive is never scored or judged. When you act on it at a moment that costs you, the GM may award **Spotlight** XP (5-9). Drives that pull different ways inside one crew are where Unity Checks come from — and where the best scenes are.

> By no means should a group disagreeing with you stop you if you feel that what you are doing is absolutely the right choice: sometimes you see beyond what is happening now, and bet on the future. Be mindful, however, not to alienate your crew — they are the only thing that can save you.

## 6-7. Strain: what the world does to your head {#sec-6-7}

HP is what the world does to your body. **Strain** (0–10, beside HP on your sheet) is what it does to your head — the weight of the century, the debt, the things you saw that shouldn't be possible (decision 266).

**What raises it.** The app does most of it: dropping below half HP (+1), a fumble or a natural 20 (+1), a crewmate going down (+2 to everyone else at the table), your debt at its worst when a mission starts (+1), and some places — the ones that shouldn't be possible — get into your head (+1, by themselves). The GM adds the rest — something hard to take (+1), something horrific (+2).

**Steady yourself.** Every gain arrives as a card: **Steady yourself** — a **Mental Strength** roll; on a success the gain is halved (rounded down, so +1 becomes nothing) — or **Take it**.

**What it does:**

| Strain | State | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 | **Calm** | — |
| 4–6 | **Shaken** | −1 TN on skill checks |
| 7–9 | **Fraying** | −2 TN on skill checks; the GM may let you notice things the others don't |
| 10 | **Breaking point** | roll a d6 (or type your die): freeze, lash out, bolt, hear your name in the static, lose ten minutes — or a **Scar**, a lasting mark rolled on its own d6. Then Strain drops to 6. |

Attacks are not affected: under fire, training takes over. **Scars** stay on your sheet (under Strain) until the GM says otherwise.

**What brings it down.** A **safe moment** — the GM's new scene with time to breathe: −1 to everyone. A crewmate's **Steady** (*Steady a crewmate* under their Strain): −1, or −2 when the crew is *Tight*; it costs an action in a fight, and a *Fractured* crew can't do it under fire. **Downtime**: a few days −2, a few weeks −4, about a month −6, several months −10 — and a better home clears more (+1 for each tier above a tenement room).

Whether what you saw was real, the game never says.

## 6-8. Falls, air and water {#sec-6-8}

The city is vertical and badly maintained: stairwells without rails, flooded basements, rooms where the air has gone bad. These rules cover what the environment does to a body when nobody is shooting.

Anything else that hurts — a pane of glass, a live casing, a chemical splash — arrives the same way: a window on your sheet that says what it costs, and, when the GM allows one, a roll **to avoid it** or **to halve it**. That roll never costs an action.

### Falling {#sec-6-8-fall}

- **Up to 3 m** (a floor, 2 squares): no damage — roll **Agility & Athletics** to land on your feet; a failure leaves you **Prone**.
- **Higher:** **1d6 per full 3 m** (6 m: 2d6, 9 m: 3d6, 15 m: 5d6), at most 10d6.
- **Landing it:** roll **Agility & Athletics** — a success **halves** the damage (rounded down). A failure takes it all and leaves you **Prone**; a **natural 20** also cripples a leg.
- **Then armor applies**, like on any hit. Cover never does.

Being pushed or thrown off something works the same way. At the table the GM sends the fall from your card (*⤓ Fall…*) with the dice already rolled: your sheet shows what landing it would save, and you roll or just take it.

### Air {#sec-6-8-air}

Smoke, gas, a flooded corridor, a sealed room: when you can't breathe, you hold it.

- **You last CON rounds** — about a minute — if you saw it coming; **half that** (rounded down) if it caught you by surprise.
- **Then a CON roll every round**, at **−1, then −2, −3…** A failure and you **black out** (*Incapacitated*).
- **Out, with no air:** **1d6 a round**, straight off HP — armor never helps — until someone drags you out.
- **Air back:** you come round, coughing (if you still have HP).

In a fight a round passes at the start of each of your turns; out of one, the table decides (*A round passes* on your sheet). Holding your breath in gas means the gas can't get in — until you have to breathe. *🫁 Hold your breath* is in your Health card; a **Submerged** zone starts it by itself.

### Water {#sec-6-8-water}

- **High water** (waist- to chest-deep): you wade or swim — **every square costs 2**, like any difficult ground. No damage by itself.
- **Submerged:** the same, and you're **holding your breath** from the moment you go under; coming up gives you air. Your sheet counts the rounds.

Both show on your map like other hazards, when the GM shows them.

## 6-9. Chases {#sec-6-9}

Somebody runs — through markets, stairwells, traffic — and the question is distance, not damage. A chase is an **abstract track**: the **gap**, from **0** (caught) to **6** (you've lost them). It usually starts at 3.

A chase runs in **legs** — one stretch of running each. In every leg:

1. **The GM frames the stretch** — and maybe a **complication** (a crowded market, a shutter coming down, a glass door that doesn't open).
2. **Each of you picks one move** and makes **one roll**. It counts twice: for its own effect, and towards **keeping pace**.

| Move | Roll | If it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Run** | Agility & Athletics | you keep pace |
| **Lead** | Streetwise or Perception | you keep pace, and the crew gets **+2** next leg |
| **Cover fire** | your weapon (a burst spent) | you keep pace; the pursuers get **−2** next leg — and their fire comes to you first |
| **Hack** | Computers & Hacking, or a Hacker's Class TN | you keep pace; their **drone** goes down, or their feeds and autopilots stumble (**−2** next leg) |
| **Trick** | Deception, Sleight of Hand or Mechanics | you keep pace; a stall over, a shutter down — a complication for *them* |
| **Help** | the skill that fits | a crewmate's roll this leg gets **+2** on a success |
| **Hide** | Stealth — only from gap 3, with no drone overhead | if everyone hides and half succeed, you're gone |

3. **The pursuers act**: they chase (one roll for the pack; faster ones get +2), or **stop and shoot** — then they don't close the gap, but whoever gave cover fire (or failed) may be hit.
4. **The gap moves.** The crew keeps pace when **at least half** of you succeed:

| | They fall back | They keep up |
|---|---|---|
| **You keep pace** | gap **+1** (+2 if everyone made it) | it holds |
| **You don't** | it holds — and the complication bites whoever failed | gap **−1** (−2 if they did it easily) |

**Falling behind.** Fail two legs in a row and the GM may leave you behind — someone will have to come back for you. **Tiring.** From the **5th leg**, every failure also costs **+1 Strain** and a **CON roll** (fail it and you're *Slowed*). At some point, turning to fight is a choice.

**The end.** Gap 6, or a successful Hide: you've lost them. Gap 0: caught — it's a fight, on the map. A dead end, a locked gate, a rooftop edge: the GM can stop the chase there and take it to a tactical map.

At the table the chase shows on top of your sheet: the track, the stretch, and your move buttons for this leg.

## 6-10. The case: dossier and board {#sec-6-10}

At the table your sheet has a **Case** tab: one per mission, the current one first, past ones to browse, and a **General** one for what runs through the whole campaign.

**The dossier** is what the crew knows for sure, kept by the GM: the **objectives** (the main one always; a secondary one when the GM tells you about it — doing it doesn't reveal it), the **pictures, documents, puzzles and messages** sent to everyone during the mission, and the **people and places** the GM has revealed. Nobody has to ask "who got the photo?" again.

**The board** is yours: a case file the crew builds — cards on a grid (a name, a place, a clue, a lead, a question, a document; three sizes; a picture on the big ones — from the dossier, or a handout you got yourself, even a private one: pinning it shares it), and **links** between them with a word on the string (*paid*, *lied*, *same van*). One of you **holds the pen** and edits it — add (or **📌 pin** a dossier entry straight onto the board), move, link, edit, unpin (unpinned cards wait in a drawer, nothing is lost); everyone else reads it and sends **✋ suggestions** to whoever holds the pen. The pen goes round: pass it to a crewmate, or pick it up when nobody has it. The GM sees it all, and sometimes a card turns up marked **anonymous tip** — whether to trust it is up to you.
